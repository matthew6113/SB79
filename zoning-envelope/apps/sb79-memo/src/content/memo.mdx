<Step id="introduction">

SB 79 requires cities to allow taller, denser housing near qualifying rail and bus stops than many local codes do. It has applied to cities since {date(L.operative.value)}.[^operative] Whether it reaches a given lot turns on a chain of map questions: which county, which city, which stop, how far away, and what the lot is zoned and holds. This explainer follows that chain from the whole state down to a single lot in Berkeley, then shows on a model of the lot what the city must allow and what a project must give in return. It cites the statute throughout, and the summaries by Coblentz Patch Duffy & Bass[^coblentz] and Holland & Knight[^hk] where they help.

</Step>

## Key Points

<Step id="key-points">

- **Where it applies.** On sites zoned for residential, mixed, or commercial use within {miles(L.half.value)} of a Tier 1 or Tier 2 transit stop in an urban transit county, or within {miles(L.quarter.value)} in cities of fewer than {num(L.pop.value)} people.[^kp-where]
- **What the city must allow.** The city may not set height or density limits below state floors, or block a residential floor area ratio up to a state figure. The floors depend on the stop's tier and the site's distance from it, from at least {ft(range.heightFt.min)} and {duac(range.densityDuPerAcre.min)} to at least {ft(range.heightFt.max)} and {duac(range.densityDuPerAcre.max)}.[^table] They are floors on the city's limits, not caps on a project.
- **What a project must do.** Include at least {word(L.cond.minUnits.value)} units at a density of at least {duac(L.cond.minDensityDuPerAcre.value)}, averaging no more than {num(L.cond.maxAverageUnitSizeNetSqFt.value)} net habitable square feet, and set aside affordable units if it has more than {word(L.aff.appliesAboveUnits.value)}. SB 79 is unavailable on a site of more than {word(L.anti.rentControlledSite.moreThanUnits.value)} units where the project would demolish rent-controlled housing occupied by tenants in the past {word(L.anti.rentControlledSite.tenantLookbackYears.value)} years.[^kp-project]
- **How projects are approved.** Through the city's usual review or SB 35 streamlining. A compliant project is deemed consistent with local plans, and from {date(L.approval.denialPresumption.beginsOn.value)} a city that denies such a project in a high-resource area is presumed to violate the Housing Accountability Act.[^kp-approval]
- **What cities can change.** A city may exempt some areas, exclude some sites for a time, or replace the defaults with an alternative plan, each subject to HCD review.[^options] San Francisco has replaced them; Berkeley has not.[^hcdsf][^yimby]
- **The example.** At {B.address}, Berkeley must allow at least {ft(B.floors.heightFt)} and {duac(B.floors.densityDuPerAcre)}, and may not block a residential floor area ratio of up to {far(B.floors.residentialFar)}, if the project meets SB 79's conditions.[^short]

</Step>

## Where does SB 79 apply?

<Step id="transit-county">

SB 79 reaches only urban transit counties: counties with more than {num(L.utc.value)} passenger rail stations.[^utc] The statute names no counties. HCD's advisory lists {word(derived.utcCountyCount)}, Alameda among them;[^hcd] Coblentz's summary also lists Orange County.[^coblentz]

<OpenIssue title="Orange County">
ABAG's summary adds Orange County, citing legislative analysis; HCD's list does not include it.[^abag] The difference does not affect Alameda. This explainer follows HCD.
</OpenIssue>

</Step>

## Can a city change the defaults?

<Step id="alternative-plans">

A city can change where and how SB 79 applies, subject to HCD review.[^review] It may exempt areas with no walking path to the stop shorter than {miles(L.options.exemptions.noWalkingPathUnderMiles.value)}, or large industrial employment hubs.[^exempt] Until {word(L.options.exclusions.untilYearsAfterSeventhHousingElement.value)} year after its seventh housing element, it may exclude listed kinds of sites, among them {L.options.exclusions.veryHighFireZoneSites.value}, {L.options.exclusions.seaLevelRiseSites.value}, and {L.historicExclusion.value}.[^exclude] Or it may replace the defaults with a transit-oriented development alternative plan.[^alt-replace] Holland & Knight reports that most cities reached first for temporary exclusions and treated alternative plans as a longer-term tool.[^hk] HCD's letters on alternative plans include {X.altPlans.sanFrancisco.name}'s, found in substantial compliance, and {X.altPlans.beverlyHills.name}'s, which HCD said needs revisions to comply;[^hcdsf][^hcdbh] both cities are grayed on the map.

</Step>

<Step id="san-francisco">

An alternative plan may move SB 79's capacity around, but not shrink it. It must keep at least the same total zoned capacity, in units and in residential floor area, across the city's transit zones;[^alt1] it may not cut any site's density, or any zone's capacity, by more than {pct(L.options.alternativePlan.maxSiteDensityCutPercent.value)};[^alt2] and no site may count for more than {pct(L.options.alternativePlan.maxSiteCapacityCountedPercent.value)} of what SB 79 would allow it.[^alt4] San Francisco's plan covers the whole city. Its planners counted about {num(X.altPlans.sanFrancisco.capacity.sb79Units)} net units of capacity under SB 79's defaults and {num(X.altPlans.sanFrancisco.capacity.planUnits)} under the plan,[^sfexec] and HCD found the plan in substantial compliance on {date(X.altPlans.sanFrancisco.hcd.date)}, on the condition that the city not enforce a clause barring density bonus projects on plan sites.[^hcdsf] The city now says no parcel in San Francisco is subject to SB 79's defaults.[^sfbulletin] HCD found Beverly Hills's plan short on the first rule: it gave some sites twice SB 79's density without more height or floor area, so its capacity did not hold up in both units and floor area.[^hcdbh]

<OpenIssue title="A pending lawsuit">
Housing groups sued San Francisco and HCD on {date(X.altPlans.sanFrancisco.lawsuit.filed)}, challenging the city's industrial-hub exclusions, its plan, and HCD's approval.[^lawsuit] This explainer describes the plan as HCD approved it.
</OpenIssue>

</Step>

## Where does SB 79 apply in Berkeley?

<Step id="berkeley">

Berkeley has {word(X.berkeleyStations.length)} BART stations, and BART is heavy rail, so all are Tier 1 stops.[^tiers][^hcd] The band from {miles(L.quarter.value)} to {miles(L.half.value)} applies only in cities of at least {num(L.pop.value)} people;[^pop] Berkeley has {num(data.population.mtc)} by the count MTC used for its map and {num(data.population.census)} by the census.[^popsrc] Berkeley adopted no exemption, exclusion, or alternative plan,[^yimby] so SB 79's defaults govern around all its stations.

<OpenIssue title="Which population count">
The statute does not say which count or date applies. Both counts clear the threshold, so the choice does not change any result in this explainer.
</OpenIssue>

</Step>

<Step id="station">

Distance runs in a straight line from the nearest edge of the parcel to a pedestrian access point of the stop,[^measure] and MTC's map, which the statute presumes valid, draws the bands from North Berkeley BART's entrances.[^map][^mtcmap] Within {ft(L.adjacent.value)} of an entrance the standards are highest; they step down at {miles(L.quarter.value)} and end at {miles(L.half.value)}.[^table]

<OpenIssue title="Which access point counts">
The statute does not define "pedestrian access point." HCD's advisory treats any station entrance, platform access point, or stop location on the regional map as a pedestrian access point.[^hcd] The choice can move a parcel between bands. Exhibit A, {A.address}, is {num(A.distanceFt)} feet from North Berkeley's elevator entrance but {num(A.distanceWithoutElevatorFt)} feet from its nearest other entrance: it is within {ft(L.adjacent.value)} only if the elevator counts.
</OpenIssue>

</Step>

<Step id="zoning-class">

SB 79 applies on sites zoned for residential, mixed, or commercial development, and not on land zoned for open space or industry.[^zoning] Every district within {miles(L.half.value)} qualifies: residential districts, a mixed-use district on BART's lots, and commercial districts. Their local height limits run from {ft(Math.min(...Object.values(data.districts).map((d) => d.heightFt)))} to {ft(Math.max(...Object.values(data.districts).map((d) => d.heightFt)))}.[^staff] Only {word(X.parcels.otherZoning)} parcels in the band fall outside any district in the city's layer.

</Step>

<Step id="bart-land">

BART owns {word(X.bart.count)} parcels here, {num(X.bart.acres, 1)} acres in all, most of them the station's parking lots.[^bartparcels] SB 79 adds a rule for such land: a transit agency may adopt zoning standards for its own land in a transit-oriented development zone, and if the city's zoning does not conform within {word(L.agencyLand.becomeLocalZoningAfterYears.value)} years, the agency's standards become the local zoning for its eligible parcels.[^agency]

<OpenIssue title="BART's own land">
AB 2923 separately gives BART zoning authority on its station land, and SB 79 does not say how the statutes interact. The example lots in this explainer stay off BART land.
</OpenIssue>

</Step>

<Step id="hazards">

A project under SB 79 must meet the height, noise, and safety standards of an adopted airport land use compatibility plan, and objective fire safety standards;[^safety] a project seeking SB 35 streamlining cannot sit in a very high fire hazard severity zone;[^sb35fire] and a city may exclude such sites for a time.[^exclude-fire] Coblentz flags both rules.[^coblentz] Neither bites near North Berkeley. CAL FIRE's maps show no very high fire hazard severity zone, and no state responsibility area, within {num(X.hazards.fire.lra.radiusM)} meters of the station, which takes in the whole band;[^calfire] and Oakland airport's influence area ends about {num(X.hazards.airportMiles, 1)} miles to the south.[^aluc]

</Step>

<Step id="rent-control">

SB 79 is unavailable on a site of more than {word(L.anti.rentControlledSite.moreThanUnits.value)} units where the project would demolish rent- or price-controlled housing that tenants occupied in the past {word(L.anti.rentControlledSite.tenantLookbackYears.value)} years,[^h1] or on a site that lost more than {word(L.anti.demolishedRentControlledSite.moreThanUnits.value)} units, some of them rent-controlled, to demolition within {word(L.anti.demolishedRentControlledSite.lookbackYears.value)} years before the application.[^h2] Berkeley publishes no parcel-level rent registry, so the map estimates. Berkeley's rent ceilings do not reach units created after {date(X.rentControl.newConstruction.cutoff)},[^rso] and {num(X.parcels.rentEstimate)} parcels within {miles(L.half.value)} have more than {word(L.anti.rentControlledSite.moreThanUnits.value)} units and were built by {X.rentControl.estimate.lastYearBuilt}. Another {num(X.parcels.rentUnknownYear)} have more than {word(L.anti.rentControlledSite.moreThanUnits.value)} units and no year built in the city's layer.[^parcels]

<OpenIssue title="An estimate">
The map cannot tell whether tenants occupied a unit in the past {word(L.anti.rentControlledSite.tenantLookbackYears.value)} years, which units are exempt for other reasons, or whether a unit count is current. Year built is a year, so buildings from {X.rentControl.estimate.lastYearBuilt} are counted though some may be newer than the cutoff. Check the Rent Board's records for any particular site.
</OpenIssue>

</Step>

<Step id="historic">

A city may exclude sites with a historic resource on a local register for a time; Berkeley did not.[^exclude-historic][^yimby] Landmark status still matters: a project that would demolish a structure on a national, state, or local historic register cannot use SB 35 streamlining.[^sb35hist] The band holds {word(X.parcels.landmark)} Berkeley Landmarks and {word(X.parcels.structureOfMerit)} Structures of Merit.[^landmarks]

<OpenIssue title="Structures of Merit">
Whether a Structure of Merit is a resource on a "local register" for these rules is not settled here.
</OpenIssue>

</Step>

<Step id="high-resource">

Beginning {date(L.approval.denialPresumption.beginsOn.value)}, a city that denies a compliant project in a high-resource area is presumed to violate the Housing Accountability Act.[^presume] A high-resource area is an area marked highest or high resource on the state's opportunity map, and every tract within {miles(L.half.value)} of the station is marked highest or high.[^opp]

</Step>

<Step id="pick-parcel">

Put the layers together and most of the band is zoned to qualify, with no flag on this map: {num(X.parcels.clear)} parcels.[^parcels] The example lot is among them. {B.address} is a {num(B.lotSqFt)}-square-foot lot zoned {B.district} ({B.districtName}), {num(B.distanceFt)} feet from the nearest station entrance{B.nearestIsElevator ? ', an elevator,' : ''} and {num(B.distanceWithoutElevatorFt)} feet from the nearest other entrance: within {miles(L.quarter.value)} either way.[^quarter] It holds {B.units === 1 ? 'a single home' : `${word(B.units)} units`}, built in {B.yearBuilt}.

</Step>

## What must the city allow?

<Step id="today">

Today, Berkeley's zoning caps a building on the lot at {ft(B.localHeightFt)}.[^staff] SB 79 sets floors on the city's limits: the city may not impose a height limit or a maximum density below the floor, and may not enforce standards that would physically preclude the floor's residential floor area ratio.[^qh][^qd][^qf]

</Step>

<Step id="height">

The floors depend on the stop's tier and the site's distance from it; the band from {miles(L.quarter.value)} to {miles(L.half.value)} counts only in cities of at least {num(L.pop.value)} people.[^table]

<StandardsTable />

The example lot is within {miles(L.quarter.value)} of a Tier 1 stop, so SB 79 bars Berkeley from imposing any height limit below {ft(B.floors.heightFt)} there,[^qh] {ft(B.heightGainFt)} above the local ceiling.

</Step>

<Step id="density">

The density floor times the lot's area gives the fewest units the city must allow. Berkeley may not impose a maximum density below {duac(B.floors.densityDuPerAcre)} on the example lot.[^qd] The lot is {num(B.acres, 3)} acres, so the city must allow at least {num(B.unitFloor, 2)} units.

<OpenIssue title="Rounding">
SB 79 does not say how a fractional unit count rounds. The Density Bonus Law rounds its own calculations up,[^round] but that rule governs bonus calculations, not the SB 79 floor. This explainer shows the unrounded figure.
</OpenIssue>

</Step>

<Step id="residential-far">

Floor area works the same way. Residential floor area counts net habitable space, finished and heated, against the area of the lot.[^rfar] Berkeley may not enforce any standard that would physically preclude a residential floor area ratio of up to {far(B.floors.residentialFar)} on the example lot,[^qf] which comes to {sqft(B.residentialFloorAreaSqFt)} of residential floor area.

</Step>

<Step id="local-rules">

Local standards still apply if they do not, alone or in concert, prevent a project from reaching those numbers.[^prevent] A rear setback, for example, may apply only so far as the project can still reach its height, density, and floor area.

<OpenIssue title={'What "prevent" means'}>
Which local standards survive is contestable, and the setback is an illustration, not a ruling. From {date(law.acts.sb1361.effective.value)}, this rule moves to <Law of={nextLaw.localStandards.mayNotPrevent} /> without change to its text.
</OpenIssue>

</Step>

## What must a project do to qualify?

<Step id="minimums">

The floors come with conditions. A project must include at least {word(L.cond.minUnits.value)} units[^units] at a density of at least {duac(L.cond.minDensityDuPerAcre.value)} or any higher local minimum,[^mindensity] and its units may average no more than {num(L.cond.maxAverageUnitSizeNetSqFt.value)} net habitable square feet.[^unitsize] On the example lot the minimum density works out to just {num(P.minDensityUnits, 2)} units, so the {word(L.cond.minUnits.value)}-unit minimum governs, and a project that used all {sqft(B.residentialFloorAreaSqFt)} of residential floor area would need at least {num(P.unitsToFillFloorArea, 2)} units to stay under the average.

</Step>

<Step id="affordability">

A project of more than {word(L.aff.appliesAboveUnits.value)} units must reserve {pct(L.aff.options.extremelyLow.value)} of its units for extremely low income households, {pct(L.aff.options.veryLow.value)} for very low income households, or {pct(L.aff.options.lower.value)} for lower income households,[^afford] and a local inclusionary rule that asks for more applies in its place.[^local] At the floor, the example lot holds {word(P.wholeUnits)} whole units, so SB 79's own rule does not reach it. Berkeley's does: its ordinance requires {pct(data.inclusionary.percentOfUnits.value)} of the units in a new housing project to be affordable, rounded up, at least {pct(data.inclusionary.veryLowShareOfRequired.value)} of them for very low income households, or an in-lieu fee.[^bmc] That is {word(P.berkeleyAtFloor)} of {word(P.wholeUnits)} units. At {word(P.nextUnits)} units, SB 79's options would ask for {num(P.sb79AtNext.extremelyLow, 2)}, {num(P.sb79AtNext.veryLow, 2)}, or {num(P.sb79AtNext.lower, 2)} units, and Berkeley's {word(P.berkeleyAtNext)} would apply instead.

<OpenIssue title="Small projects and Berkeley's rule">
SB 79 does not say whether a local inclusionary rule reaches a project too small for its own affordability rule; this explainer shows Berkeley's applying. If the unit floor rounded up, the lot would reach SB 79's rule as well. Berkeley's percentages come from a mirror of the Municipal Code; the official site was not reachable for this explainer. In applying SB 79's own options, this explainer counts a unit at a deeper income level toward the shallower options too.
</OpenIssue>

</Step>

<Step id="counterfactual">

A single failed condition ends the inquiry. The example lot holds {B.units === 1 ? 'a single home' : `${word(B.units)} units`}, so the rent-control bar does not reach it.[^h1] Had it held {word(derived.rentControlledSiteUnits)} units, and had the project required demolishing rent-controlled housing occupied by tenants within the past {word(L.anti.rentControlledSite.tenantLookbackYears.value)} years, SB 79 would not apply and local zoning would govern. The same holds for a mobilehome, RV, or special occupancy park.[^h3]

</Step>

## How does SB 79 work with the density bonus?

<Step id="density-bonus">

SB 79's density becomes the base density for the State Density Bonus Law.[^dbase] Bonus units stack on top of it; extra height does not. A city need not grant height beyond SB 79's floor through the bonus law,[^dheight] so on the example lot the height stays at {ft(B.floors.heightFt)}, except that an all-affordable project near transit may gain up to {ft(L.bonus.heightExceptionFt.value)}.[^dexc] A project that reaches {duac(L.q.concessionMinDensityDuPerAcre.value)} there and otherwise qualifies earns up to {word(L.bonus.additionalConcessions.extremelyLow.value)} more concessions, depending on how deep its affordability goes.[^dconc]

</Step>

<Step id="density-bonus-contrast">

The density bonus and SB 79 can point in different directions. At {contrast.address} in Oakland, {num(contrast.distanceFt)} feet from a Rockridge BART entrance, SB 79 requires the city to allow at least {ft(contrast.sb79HeightFt)};[^qh] Oakland's zoning already allows {ft(contrast.localHeightFt)}.[^oakh] The pending application there relies on the Density Bonus Law, not SB 79, with towers of {ft(contrast.towerHeightsFt[0])} and {ft(contrast.towerHeightsFt[1])} and no affordable units,[^proj] under rules vested by a preliminary application filed {date(contrast.preliminaryApplication)}, before SB 79 reached cities on {date(L.operative.value)}.[^operative]

</OpenIssue>

</Step>

## How are projects approved?

<Step id="approval">

SB 79 creates no approval process of its own and does not change CEQA.[^noprocess] A compliant project is deemed consistent with local plans for purposes of the Housing Accountability Act,[^haa] and may seek streamlined ministerial approval under SB 35[^sb35] without SB 35's test of the city's housing production.[^sb35waive] On that path it must meet SB 35's affordability terms: {pct(L.streamlining.affordability.rentalPercent.value)} of units at or below {pct(L.streamlining.affordability.rentalAmiPercent.value)} of area median income for rentals, {pct(L.streamlining.affordability.ownershipPercent.value)} at or below {pct(L.streamlining.affordability.ownershipAmiPercent.value)} for sale, or, in the Bay Area, {pct(L.streamlining.affordability.bayAreaPercent.value)} below {pct(L.streamlining.affordability.bayAreaBelowAmiPercent.value)} averaging {pct(L.streamlining.affordability.bayAreaAverageAmiPercent.value)}.[^sb35aff] Coblentz walks through the path.[^coblentz] Beginning {date(L.approval.denialPresumption.beginsOn.value)}, a city that denies a compliant project in a high-resource area is presumed to violate the Act and is liable for fines of at least {money(L.approval.denialPresumption.minFinePerUnitUsd.value)} per unit, unless it shows a health, life, or safety reason for the denial.[^presume] The example lot lies in a {B.opportunity} area.[^opp]

</Step>

<Step id="other-bands">

Distance steps the standards down. 'above' : 'below'} the {ftAdj(L.labor.buildingHeightOverFt.value)} line over which prevailing-wage and skilled-workforce standards apply.[^labor] Exhibit C, {C.address}, lies between {miles(L.quarter.value)} and {miles(L.half.value)} out and gets at least {ft(C.floors.heightFt)}, {duac(C.floors.densityDuPerAcre)}, and a residential floor area ratio of {far(C.floors.residentialFar)}.[^outer]

Exhibit A is within {ft(L.adjacent.value)} only if the elevator entrance counts. </Step>

## Key Takeaways

<Step id="takeaways">

- **Start with the map.** County, city, stop tier, and the straight-line distance from the lot's nearest edge to an entrance decide whether SB 79 applies and which standards.[^measure]
- **Check for local changes.** Exemptions, exclusions, and alternative plans can replace the defaults, as San Francisco's plan has.[^hcdsf] Berkeley adopted none,[^yimby] so the defaults govern.
- **Then check the lot.** Zoning, BART land, landmark status, and rent-controlled housing can each take a site out, or off the streamlined path.[^h1][^sb35hist]
- **The standards are floors.** At {B.address}, a lot its own zoning caps at {ft(B.localHeightFt)}, Berkeley must allow at least {ft(B.floors.heightFt)}, {duac(B.floors.densityDuPerAcre)}, and a residential floor area ratio of {far(B.floors.residentialFar)}: a floor of {num(B.unitFloor, 2)} units and {sqft(B.residentialFloorAreaSqFt)} of residential floor area.[^short]
- **The law is still moving.** Amendments take effect on {date(law.acts.ab2576.effective.value)},[^amend] cities are still adopting plans, and several questions remain open. This explainer states the law as of {date(asOf)}.

</Step>
